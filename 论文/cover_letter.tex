Dear Editors,

\noindent
Please consider our manuscript, ``The Boussinesq--compressible gap in steady two-dimensional
buoyancy-driven enclosure convection is linear to leading order in the relative temperature
contrast: a factorised physics prior for surrogates'', for publication in the \emph{International Journal of
Heat and Mass Transfer} as a full-length article.

\noindent
Surrogates for thermal convection are trained overwhelmingly on Boussinesq data, yet the flows
engineers need are compressible. The two are close in integral quantities such as the Nusselt
number and differ in the local fields that surrogates are asked to reproduce. The manuscript
establishes what sets the size of that difference and turns the answer into a design rule.

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item \textbf{One axis.} In the non-dimensionalisation used by the released paired data and by our
solver, the Froude number is pinned ($Fr^2=\varepsilon$ identically) and the Mach number enters at
$O(Ma^2)$; a 24-fold change in $Ma$ leaves the measured correction unchanged at the precision of
the solve. The
break-symmetry group is therefore one-dimensional, spanned by $\varepsilon=\Delta T/T_0$.
\item \textbf{A measured law.} Across 627 paired two-dimensional solves in the unit square at
$Pr=0.71$ spanning $\varepsilon=0.02$--$1.9$, the relative correction obeys
$R=A\varepsilon^{m}$ with $m=1.02$ in $u_x$ and $u_y$. The pressure channel gives $m=1.01$ in a
fixed post-processing gauge; it is a numerical observation, not a gauge-invariant exponent.
$\theta$ is a weak, flow-dependent channel whose free power-law fit is not identifiable
($R^2=0.22$; slope 1.02 over the full window, 1.67 on the 104-configuration subset), so we make no
exponent claim for it. The intercept vanishes and the
$\varepsilon^2$ curvature matches the asymptotic expansion. In 104-sample checks in a 2:1 geometry
(0.7--1.8 standard errors) and at $Pr=4.38$ (1.5\%) the $u_x/u_y$ exponent is consistent; the median
correction reproduces on an independent public data set at $\varepsilon=1.2$.
\item \textbf{A design rule.} Because the correction factorises as $D=\varepsilon P(X)$, a network
that predicts only $P$ has a $1.09\times$ lower channel-averaged error than the strongest
conditional baseline in extrapolation ($\theta$ and $p$ individually, $p$ in the fixed gauge; the $u_x/u_y$ intervals
include zero), and $1.87\times$ better (channel-averaged; $1.89\times$ in $u_x$) than an
$\varepsilon$-blind network when $\varepsilon$ is
withheld, which makes
single-contrast public data reusable at other contrasts within one boundary-condition family, without
retraining.
\item \textbf{A consistency check on the linearised operator.} The factorisation is not only a fit. One Newton step of the
low-Mach residual at the Boussinesq state, on the same discretisation, reproduces the measured
correction to median $4.7/3.8/7.2\%$ ($u_x/u_y/p$) on 27 samples at $\varepsilon=0.02$--$0.10$,
with no extrapolation in $\varepsilon$; and the extrapolated shape predicts held-out measurements
at $\varepsilon=0.1$ and $0.2$ to $2.4/1.8/3.8\%$.
\item \textbf{Wall heat transfer.} At $\varepsilon=1.2$ the integral Nusselt number changes by
$0.67\%$ (mean per-sample ratio $0.54\%$, median $0.58\%$), while the local wall heat flux is
redistributed by $4.25\%$; the integral agreement
therefore conceals a local error that a surrogate must be trained to resolve. A prior trained on
the public data cuts the temperature-field error from $7.2\%$ to $0.10\%$ and the local wall-flux
error to $4.3\%$, but its integrated wall-$Nu$ error is $2.6\%$, against $0.7\%$ for doing
nothing; the wall-normal derivative, not the field norm, is the limiting quantity for a heat-flux
surrogate.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
IJHMT has published numerical methodology for non-Oberbeck--Boussinesq flows (Demou, Frantzis and
Grigoriadis, \emph{Int.\ J.\ Heat Mass Transfer} 125 (2018) 1156--1168), so the present study fits
the journal's scope: it quantifies that effect and turns it into a surrogate design rule.

\noindent
The main sweep is two-dimensional and steady; a preliminary check on fifteen paired three-dimensional samples
spanning five contrasts reproduces the exponent, and a POD-ROM with at most four modes on the same samples lowers the mean $u_x$
error from 0.168 to 0.081 over five held-out contrasts. We state the remaining limits (one Rayleigh number,
$res=16$, $\varepsilon\le1.6$) explicitly. Every number in
the paper is regenerated from stored solver output by the scripts in the repository; the public
data set of the companion study is used unchanged, and our scripts and machine-readable results are
released with the manuscript; the raw solver output is available from the authors on request.
