\title{The Boussinesq--compressible gap in steady two-dimensional buoyancy-driven
enclosure convection is linear to leading order in the relative temperature contrast:\\
a factorised physics prior for surrogates}

\author[a]{Author(s)}
\address[a]{Affiliation(s)}

\begin{abstract}
Boussinesq-trained surrogates for thermal convection transfer poorly across temperature contrast:
they learn one correction field and have no signal with which to rescale it. For steady
two-dimensional buoyancy-driven convection in enclosures we show that the contrast
$\varepsilon=\Delta T/T_0$ sets the leading-order scaling of the difference between the Boussinesq
and compressible solutions, while the flow sets its amplitude and spatial shape. The Froude number
is tied to the contrast ($Fr^2=\varepsilon$), and $Ma$ enters only at $O(Ma^2)$. Over 627 paired
solutions spanning $\varepsilon=0.02$--$1.9$, the relative correction obeys $R=A\varepsilon^m$ with
$m=1.023\pm0.018$ and $0.995\pm0.019$ for $(u_x,u_y)$, a vanishing intercept and $\varepsilon^2$
curvature; the pressure channel gives $m=1.009\pm0.015$ in a fixed gauge, and $\theta$ has no
identifiable exponent. The correction factorises as $D=\varepsilon P(X)$, so a surrogate can learn
only the spatial shape $P$ and multiply by $\varepsilon$ outside the network. When only a
single-$\varepsilon$ data set exists, it lowers the channel-averaged transfer error from 0.131 to
0.086 ($1.5\times$) and makes that data set reusable within a boundary family. At $\varepsilon=1.2$ on the
public classical-cavity data, the integral Nusselt number changes by $0.54\%$ while the local wall flux changes by $4.25\%$. One inference
takes 6.3\,ms; replacing the compressible member saves $2.9\times$ per pair and $13\times$ for a
20-flow $\times$ 5-contrast scan. A 15-sample, five-contrast 3-D check gives per-flow exponents
within 0.16 of unity.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Steady natural convection is a workhorse of thermal engineering and it is expensive. A converged
pair of two-dimensional solves on a $64^2$ mesh costs 34\,s of wall clock on an eight-way desktop
(Sec.~\ref{sec:cost}); the three-dimensional problems that engineering actually needs cost hours to
days. That is the standard motivation for surrogate modelling, and the standard response is to
train a network that maps a cheap model's output to an expensive model's output
\citep{giacomini2026surrogates,meng2025physics}.

For convection the two models are not two arbitrary fidelities: they are the Boussinesq
(Oberbeck--Boussinesq) equations and the compressible low-Mach equations. Boussinesq is exact only
in the limit of vanishing relative temperature contrast; the corrections are $O(\beta\Delta T)$ and
have been characterised asymptotically for decades \citep{spiegel1960boussinesq,gray1976validity}.
Recent work makes the pair directly learnable. \citet{menglik2026neural} publish paired
Boussinesq$\to$compressible steady solutions in two dimensions (4000 training pairs plus held-out
splits, CC BY 4.0) and train a Fourier neural operator \citep{li2021fourier} to predict the
compressible field from the Boussinesq field. That data set---which we use as an independent
validation set in Secs.~\ref{sec:public}--\ref{sec:transfer}---contains exactly one
temperature contrast: $\Delta T=1.2$ at $T_0=1$.

The single-contrast design is the practical crux. A surrogate fitted on such a data set has learned
the correction at one operating point. At any other contrast the Boussinesq input is unchanged---$X$
is $\varepsilon$-blind---while the correction that must be added scales with $\varepsilon$; the
network therefore has no signal with which to rescale the correction it memorised, and the only
remedy on offer is to generate a second paired data set, i.e.\ to pay again exactly the cost the
surrogate was meant to remove. We show in Sec.~\ref{sec:floor} that this is not a modelling failure
that more capacity or a better architecture can repair: the missing variable is $\varepsilon$, and
we measure the resulting ceiling.

This paper makes the barrier explicit and then removes it.

\paragraph{What governs the gap} For the non-dimensionalisation used here the Froude number is not
free: $Fr^2=\beta\Delta T=\Delta T/T_0$ identically, and the Mach number enters the equations only
at $O(Ma^2)$ within the retained low-Mach equations (Sec.~\ref{sec:axis}; a factor-24 change in
$Ma$ leaves the measured correction unchanged at the precision of the solve). The
break-symmetry group is therefore one-dimensional, and it has a
name: $\varepsilon=\Delta T/T_0$.

\paragraph{What the gap looks like} Expanding the compressible member in $\varepsilon$ gives an
$O(1)$ system that is term-by-term the Boussinesq system, plus an $O(\varepsilon)$ source produced
by the density-modulated inertia and by the non-solenoidal velocity field
$\nabla\cdot\bm{u}_1=\nabla\cdot(\theta_0\bm{u}_0)$. Because the linearised operator is
$\varepsilon$-independent, the correction factorises,
$Y-X=\varepsilon P(X)+\varepsilon^2Q(X)+\dots$, with a shape $P$ that does not rotate as
$\varepsilon$ moves (Sec.~\ref{sec:theory}). Measured over two decades of $\varepsilon$ (627
samples, Sec.~\ref{sec:law}) the relative correction obeys $R=A\varepsilon^m$ with
$m=1.023\pm0.018$ for $u_x$ and $0.995\pm0.019$ for $u_y$. The pressure channel gives $m=1.01$ in the fixed gauge of
Sec.~\ref{sec:pressure}, a numerical observation rather than a gauge-invariant exponent. $\theta$
is a weak, flow-dependent channel: its free power-law fit is not identifiable ($R^2=0.22$; slope
1.02 over the full window, 1.67 on the 104-configuration subset), so we make no exponent claim for
it; its amplitude is set by the flow, with the $\chi$ parameterisation at $R^2=0.78$ and the
$\hat u$ form at $0.90$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:law}). The exponent is
window-averaged: the same 627 points give $1.23$--$1.32$ when fitted from $\varepsilon=0.8$
upwards, which is the $\varepsilon^2$ curvature. The intercept vanishes, and the prefactor's weak
Rayleigh-number trend reproduces in a second geometry and in a third Prandtl number.

\paragraph{What it buys} Because the $\varepsilon$ dependence can be written outside the network, a
surrogate can predict $P(X)$ and multiply by $\varepsilon$ afterwards. Supplying $\varepsilon$ as
an input as well gives the largest gain relative to the strongest conditional baseline:
$1.07\times$ in $u_x$ for the linear form and $1.15\times$ for the quadratic form
(channel-averaged $1.09\times$ and $1.21\times$; the $\theta$ and $p$ channels are individually
significant, $p$ in the fixed gauge, while the $u_x$ and $u_y$ intervals include zero;
Sec.~\ref{sec:prior})---while removing it from
the input raises the extrapolation error from 0.0937 to 0.1214 in $u_x$ and from 0.1126 to 0.1682 in
$p$ (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}), but that removal
is what lets a network trained on a public data set that exists at a single $\varepsilon$ be
applied, without retraining, to our $\varepsilon\in[0.2,1.9]$, where it reaches
$\mathrm{relL2}=0.093$ ($u_x$) against $0.144$ for the plain model at the same budget
(Sec.~\ref{sec:transfer}). The prior is also the cheaper model when data is scarce
(Sec.~\ref{sec:learning}: $1.4\times$ better than an $\varepsilon$-augmented baseline at $n=50$,
crossover near $n\approx300$), the fitted curvature of its quadratic form is positive and of the magnitude the expansion
predicts ($\kappa=0.22$--$0.42$ in all four channels), and the same prior transfers
unchanged to a Fourier neural operator.

\subsection{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

Reduced-order models replace the discretised equations by a small number of modes; parametric
dynamic mode decomposition is a representative recent example \citep{huhn2022parametric}. Graph
convolutional networks exploit the mesh and have been applied to natural convection in annuli
\citep{feng2024graph} and to geometry-adaptive steady convection with physics-informed losses
\citep{peng2023physics}. Operator learning removes the mesh-resolution constraint: DeepONet
approximates the solution operator from branch and trunk networks \citep{lu2021learning},
physics-informed DeepONets add the residual to the loss \citep{wang2021learning}, and Fourier
neural operators (FNOs) learn a global convolution in Fourier space \citep{li2021fourier}. FNOs
have since been used for sparse-field reconstruction \citep{zhao2023recfno}, real-time heat-pipe
startup prediction \citep{yu2026fourier}, and operator-level uncertainty quantification in
cooling-channel optimisation \citep{sahin2023deep}. Physics-informed neural networks
\citep{raissi2019physics} and their heat-transfer variants \citep{cai2021physics,jalili2023physics}
take the opposite route: they solve one instance by minimising a PDE residual rather than learning
a family of instances. Reviews of the numerical analysis and of the application landscape are
given by \citet{deryck2024numerical}, \citet{meng2025physics} and \citet{luo2025physics}.

A physics prior can enter as a residual penalty, as a conservation law built into the architecture,
or as an algebraic factorisation of the output. The first two are well developed; the third is the
subject of this paper. Residual-based corrector operators solve a linear variational problem for
the residual of a trained operator and add the solution to its prediction; the correction is
deterministic, costs one linear solve per query, and improves accuracy without retraining the base
operator \citep{jha2023residual,cao2023residual}. Physics-informed extrapolation of deep operators
has been demonstrated by adding sparse observations or residual terms \citep{zhu2023reliable}, and
transfer operator learning addresses distribution shift between parameter regimes
\citep{goswami2022deep}. Multi-fidelity surrogates combine cheap and expensive data through LSTM
corrections \citep{conti2022multi}, feature-adjacent physics-informed learning
\citep{chen2023feature} or transfer-learned FNOs \citep{lyu2023multi}. These methods reduce the
amount of high-fidelity data, but they still learn the parameter dependence from samples. For
buoyant convection, the Oberbeck--Boussinesq approximation and its validity limits are classical
\citep{spiegel1960boussinesq,gray1976validity}, and the review of \citet{ahlers2009heat}
summarises the heat-transfer and large-scale dynamics of turbulent Rayleigh--B\'enard convection.
Non-Oberbeck--Boussinesq (NOB) effects have been measured under strong temperature contrast
\citep{yik2020turbulent}, in cryogenic helium \citep{macek2023assessing} and in a water-filled
differentially heated cavity \citep{pan2023non}. At $Pr=0.71$ and large contrast they are not a
small correction: they grow cold-corner vortices and can reverse the large-scale circulation
\citep{xia2016flow}. On the numerical side, low-Mach compressible formulations for enclosed thermal
convection have been developed and compared with the Boussinesq limit
\citep{zhang2023investigation,satake2024mathematical}, and the rigorous derivation of the
Oberbeck--Boussinesq approximation has recently been revisited \citep{bella2023rigorous}. Bella
et al.\ obtain the Oberbeck--Boussinesq system with a non-local boundary condition for the
temperature deviation in the low-Mach limit of the compressible Navier--Stokes--Fourier system with
Dirichlet temperature conditions; that boundary condition belongs to the full
compressible-to-Oberbeck--Boussinesq limit, whereas
the expansion here is taken in the retained low-Mach system with fixed wall temperature, where the
temperature deviation vanishes at every order on the walls. What is
missing is a quantitative, closed-form account of how the \emph{difference between the two
solutions} scales with the temperature contrast, and a surrogate design that uses that scaling.
These studies fix $\varepsilon$ and compare the two solutions; none extracts the $\varepsilon$
dependence as a reusable prior. The present work differs in that the parameter dependence is
derived, and only the spatial shape of the correction is learned.

\section{Problem, discretisation and the break-symmetry parameter}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Two members per sample}
\label{sec:pair}

Every sample is a \emph{paired} steady solution of one physical configuration with two equation sets
on the same mesh ($res=64$, $L=1$, $Pr=0.71$, $\gamma=1.4$). The high-fidelity member $Y$ is the
compressible low-Mach formulation in $\bm w=(\bm u,T,\rho,p)$:
\begin{align}
\nabla\cdot(\rho\bm u)&=0,\label{eq:cmass}\\
\nabla\cdot(\rho\,\bm u\otimes\bm u)&=-\nabla p+\frac{1}{Re}\nabla\cdot\bm\sigma-\frac{1}{Fr^2}\rho\bm g,\label{eq:cmom}\\
\nabla\cdot(\rho E\bm u)&=\frac{\gamma}{Re\,Pr}\nabla^2e+\nabla\cdot(\bm P_{\mathrm{work}}\bm u)+O(Ma^2),\label{eq:cenergy}\\
\rho e&=\gamma Ma^2p+1,\qquad Re=(Ra/Pr)^{1/2}.\label{eq:ceos}
\end{align}
The low-fidelity member $X$ is the Boussinesq system in $(\bm u,T,p)$, written in the \emph{same}
free-fall scales:
\begin{align}
\nabla\cdot\bm u&=0,\label{eq:bmass}\\
\bm u\cdot\nabla\bm u&=-\nabla p+\left(\frac{Pr}{Ra}\right)^{1/2}\nabla^2\bm u+\theta\hat{\bm g},\label{eq:bmom}\\
\bm u\cdot\nabla T&=(Ra\,Pr)^{-1/2}\nabla^2T,\qquad \theta=\frac{T-T_0}{\Delta T}.\label{eq:benergy}
\end{align}
Both are solved with Taylor--Hood-type mixed elements, damped Newton iterations and a MUMPS
factorisation; steady states are reached by an adaptive march with a convergence guard. Both
members of a pair start from the same initial field and the same wall-temperature seed, so they
follow the same branch. The
temperature boundary data are random Fourier wall temperatures with per-wall mode dials; the
classical differentially heated cavity (constant hot and cold side walls, adiabatic top and
bottom) is the one-mode member of the same family. Both are buoyancy-driven enclosure flows, and
the results below are not restricted to the Rayleigh--B\'enard layer.

The generator draws $Ra$ and $\varepsilon$ independently. At fixed $Ra$, changing $\varepsilon$
changes $Fr=\sqrt{\varepsilon}$, i.e.\ the buoyancy scale, while $Re=(Ra/Pr)^{1/2}$, $Pr$ and
$\gamma$ stay fixed. The Boussinesq member therefore sees only $Ra$ and the thermal boundary data;
the compressible member sees the same flow at a different $Fr$. This is a statement about the
dimensionless parameterisation, not a claim that one fixed apparatus realises every pair: at fixed
$Ra$, changing $\varepsilon$ requires the dimensional grouping $g\beta L^3/(\nu\alpha)$ to change in
inverse proportion to $\Delta T$. A fixed apparatus follows the one-dimensional slice
$Ra\propto\varepsilon$; the $(Ra,\varepsilon)$ rectangle contains that slice but is not restricted
to it.

\subsection{The low-fidelity solution is exactly $\varepsilon$-blind}
\label{sec:blind}

$\theta$, $Pr$ and $Ra$ are the only quantities that appear in the Boussinesq weak form; neither
$T_0$ nor $\Delta T$ appears alone. Hence, with the thermal data prescribed in $\theta$ and $Ra$
fixed, $X$ cannot depend on $\varepsilon$. This was verified to solver precision: across
20 configurations $\times$ 5 values of $\varepsilon$ (same $Ra$, bit-identical $\theta$ boundary
stream), the maximum relative deviation of $X$ is $9.77\times10^{-8}$.

\begin{quote}
\noindent\textbf{Corollary.} The amplitude of the correction is set by $\varepsilon$, which is
\emph{absent from the surrogate's input}. The spatial shape of the correction is a function of $X$
and is learnable; no function of $X$ alone, however expressive, can supply the factor
$\varepsilon$ that scales it. The limit is informational, not architectural
(Sec.~\ref{sec:floor}).
\end{quote}

\section{Asymptotic theory: why the correction factorises as $\varepsilon P(X)$}
\label{sec:theory}

Write $T=T_0(1+\varepsilon\theta)$ with $\theta=O(1)$. With $\rho=1/T$ (the equation of state and
initial condition used here) and $Fr^2=\varepsilon$, the buoyancy source is
\begin{equation}
\frac{\rho\bm g}{\varepsilon}=\frac{\bm g}{\varepsilon}-\theta\bm g+\varepsilon\theta^2\bm g+\dots,
\label{eq:buoyancy}
\end{equation}
the constant $\bm g/\varepsilon$ is balanced by a hydrostatic pressure $p_{-1}/\varepsilon$ with
$\nabla p_{-1}=-\bm g$, which drops out at the next order, so the $O(1)$ balances are
\begin{align}
\nabla\cdot\bm u_0&=0,\label{eq:o1mass}\\
\bm u_0\cdot\nabla\bm u_0&=-\nabla\tilde p_0+Re^{-1}\nabla^2\bm u_0+\theta_0\hat{\bm g},\label{eq:o1mom}\\
\bm u_0\cdot\nabla\theta_0&=(Re\,Pr)^{-1}\nabla^2\theta_0,\label{eq:o1energy}
\end{align}
which is term-by-term the Boussinesq system solved as $X$ ($Re^{-1}=(Pr/Ra)^{1/2}$). The
correction at $O(\varepsilon)$ is driven by the density modulation:
\begin{align}
\nabla\cdot\bm u_1&=\nabla\cdot(\theta_0\bm u_0),\label{eq:div1}\\
\nabla\cdot(\bm u_1\otimes\bm u_0+\bm u_0\otimes\bm u_1-\theta_0\bm u_0\otimes\bm u_0)
&=-\nabla\tilde p_1+Re^{-1}\nabla^2\bm u_1+\theta_1\hat{\bm g}-\theta_0^2\hat{\bm g}.\label{eq:mom1}
\end{align}

The energy equation contributes at the next order. Writing
$T=T_0(1+\varepsilon\theta_0+\varepsilon^2\theta_1+\dots)$, the $O(\varepsilon)$ balance is the base
energy equation for $\theta_0$ already contained in $\mathcal{L}$, and $\theta_1$ enters at
$O(\varepsilon^2)$ in $T$. The relative correction in the normalised $\theta$ is still
$O(\varepsilon)$, because it is measured against $\theta_0$, whose norm is the largest of the four
fields; that normalisation, not a higher order in $\theta_1$, is why $\theta$ has the smallest
relative amplitude. The
$O(\varepsilon)$ system is linear, with coefficients fixed by $X$. Its mass equation is
inhomogeneous, so the operator is not the Boussinesq operator linearised about $X$---whose
velocities are divergence-free---but the linearisation of the retained low-Mach system about $X$,
with the mass constraint carried inside the operator. We denote that operator by $\mathcal{L}$;
its inverse, up to the constant-pressure gauge, is written $\mathcal{L}^{-1}$. It is solvable: its
only singular mode is the constant pressure, and the compatibility condition
$\int\nabla\cdot(\theta_0\bm u_0)\,d\Omega=\oint\theta_0\bm u_0\cdot\bm n\,dS=0$ holds because
$\bm u_0\cdot\bm n=0$ on the walls, so $\tilde p_1$ is determined up to the gauge.
Since $\mathcal{L}$ is built from $X$ and the retained equations, and contains no explicit
$\varepsilon$,
\begin{equation}
D=Y-X=-\varepsilon \mathcal{L}^{-1}S_1[X]-\varepsilon^2\mathcal{L}^{-1}S_2[X]+\dots
=\varepsilon P(X)+\varepsilon^2Q(X)+\dots
\label{eq:factor}
\end{equation}
Five predictions follow, and all five hold; Table~S1 of the supplementary material lists them and
their measured values. Table~S1 also measures the remainder: the $\varepsilon^1$ term carries 86\%
of the correction energy, $\varepsilon^1+\varepsilon^2$ carries 99.0\%, and
$\varepsilon^1+\varepsilon^2+\varepsilon^3$ carries 99.9\%, so the two-term truncation leaves 1.0\%
over the sampled range. This is a measured statement about the retained low-Mach system, not a
proof of convergence.



The divergence identity is not exact at the top of the range: the cosine between its two sides
falls from $0.994$ to $0.901$ as $\varepsilon$ rises to $1.9$, which is the footprint of the
$\varepsilon^2$ term. The fifth row of Table~S1 quantifies that term independently.

\paragraph{Domain of validity} $\varepsilon<2$, since the cold wall $T_c=T_0-\Delta T/2$ must be
positive. At $\varepsilon=1.9$ the cold wall is $T_c/T_0=0.05$, so the density expansion is close
to its limit.

\paragraph{Temperature normalisation} Eq.~\eqref{eq:benergy} defines the zero-mean variable
$\theta=(T-T_0)/\Delta T$. The public release and our own pipeline store $T/T_0$ and evaluate every
temperature error on the wall-normalised $\theta_w=(T-T_c)/\Delta T\in[0,1]$, which differs from
$\theta$ by the constant $1/2$. All $R_\theta$ and $A_\theta$ quoted below are in the
wall-normalised convention. The same field difference reads $0.0220$ wall-normalised against
$0.0869$ zero-mean on the public random-wall test split, and $0.0836$ against $0.1628$ on the
classical cavity; the two conventions must not be mixed. In Table~\ref{tab:law} the $\theta$
amplitude is therefore listed in both, $0.010$ wall-normalised and $0.034$ zero-mean.

\paragraph{The exponent is window-averaged, and the curvature is real} The fit above uses the full
two decades, including the 27 check points at $\varepsilon\le0.1$ where $R$ is closest to its
linear limit; these points are in the fit, not a holdout. Fitting only $\varepsilon\ge0.8$ gives
$m=1.23\pm0.08/1.25\pm0.08/1.21\pm0.34/1.32\pm0.06$, and fitting each of the 20 paired
configurations separately over $\varepsilon\in[0.4,1.9]$ gives a median
$m=1.31/1.17/1.21/1.45$ (median $R^2=0.98$). The 104 paired configurations used
for the Prandtl comparison (Sec.~\ref{sec:water}) give
$1.16/1.05/1.67/1.11$ over their full range; the $\theta$ slope is the least stable
because its signal is the weakest ($R^2=0.22$ here and $0.33$ in that subset), and it moves
much more than the velocity and pressure slopes. That is the signature of
$R=A\varepsilon(1+\kappa\varepsilon)$ rather than of an exact power: a power law fitted to a curved
function over a window that excludes small $\varepsilon$ returns $m\approx1+\kappa\langle
\varepsilon\rangle$. The quadratic fit $R=a\varepsilon+c\varepsilon^2+b$ gives $c>0$ in all four
channels ($u_x$: $+0.0513\pm0.0133$, $3.9\sigma$; $u_y$: $+0.0695$; $\theta$: $+0.0060$; $p$:
$+0.0539$) with $\kappa=c/a=0.70/0.83/0.42/0.73$ (here $a$ and $c$ are coefficients of this quadratic fit, not the power-law $A$ of Table~\ref{tab:law}). We therefore state the law as first order
in $\varepsilon$, $R=A\varepsilon(1+O(\varepsilon))$, quote $m=1.02$ as the number a wide-window
fit returns, and note that the network recovers the $\varepsilon^2$ curvature with the same sign and order of magnitude
(Sec.~\ref{sec:prior}, $\kappa=0.22$--$0.42$) and by the mode expansion (Sec.~\ref{sec:theory},
Table~S1 of the supplementary material: $\varepsilon^1+\varepsilon^2$ carry 99.0\% of the
correction energy).

\paragraph{Mesh independence} One paired configuration (the classical cavity at $Ra=10^4$) was
rerun at $128^2$ over the same five contrasts. The relative correction $R$ changes by at most
$0.1\%$ in $u_x$ and $u_y$, $0.7\%$ in $\theta$ and $1.1\%$ in $p$. Fitted over
$\varepsilon\in[0.4,1.9]$, the exponents are $1.192/1.208/1.247/1.305$ at $64^2$ and
$1.192/1.208/1.247/1.300$ at $128^2$ ($u_x/u_y/\theta/p$), so the refinement moves the exponent by
at most $0.005$. For these two configurations the exponent is not set by the $64^2$
discretisation.

\needspace{9\baselineskip}
At the top of the Rayleigh range we repeated the check for a constant-wall configuration at
$Ra=10^6$, over $\varepsilon=0.4$, $1.2$ and $1.9$. The exponents are
$1.067$, $1.129$, $1.301$ and $1.335$ at $64^2$, against $1.063$, $1.124$, $1.289$ and $1.321$
at $128^2$ (for $u_x$, $u_y$, $\theta$ and $p$), a shift of at most $0.015$. The largest pointwise change in $R$ is $3.3\%$
($p$ at $\varepsilon=1.9$); the other channels change by $1.0$--$1.9\%$. The thermal boundary layer
at $Ra=10^6$ is $\approx0.032L$ thick; the $64^2$ mesh places three temperature and five velocity
nodes across it, the $128^2$ mesh five and nine. The $128^2$ values are the finer reference: the
$64^2$ exponent is accurate to $0.015$, and an individual $\varepsilon=1.9$ correction carries a
discretisation uncertainty of up to $3.3\%$.



\begin{itemize}
\item For $u_x$, $u_y$ and $p$ the exponent is consistent across geometry within its uncertainty
(0.7--1.8 standard errors; $p$ in the fixed gauge); the
prefactor $A$ is not ($0.147\to0.175$ for $u_x$, $0.148\to0.222$ for $p$, $0.193\to0.175$ for
$u_y$, $0.010\to0.008$ for $\theta$).
\item Boundary-condition families barely matter \emph{inside this family}: 134 wall-mode families,
133 free dummies. Adding them to the $\varepsilon$-only fit raises the adjusted $R^2$ by 2.3 pp
($p$), 2.9 pp ($u_y$), 3.3 pp ($u_x$) and 8.5 pp ($\theta$)---the $\varepsilon$-scaling is not an
artefact of which wall modes were drawn.
\item The $\theta$ channel needs one extra, cheaply available parameter, the advection strength of
the base flow $\chi=\|\bm u\|\|\nabla\theta\|/\|\theta\|$:
$R_\theta=\varepsilon(-0.0037+0.226\chi)$ with the $\varepsilon$ factor imposed, not fitted;
$R^2$ $0.185\to0.778$ (Spearman 0.902), i.e.\ $R_\theta\approx0.23\varepsilon\chi$. This is a
parameterisation of the $\theta$ amplitude, not an independent measurement of its exponent. For
$u_x/u_y/p$ adding $\chi$ does nothing. A single-input
form of the same statement, $R_\theta/\varepsilon=c\hat u^a$, is calibrated on two independent
generators in Sec.~\ref{sec:public} and carries the same information with $R^2=0.90$.
\item The fitted $\theta$ amplitude is not predictive: $A_\theta=0.010$ with $m=1.024$ gives $0.012$
at $\varepsilon=1.2$, while the median $R_\theta$ on the public data is $0.0228$. The factor
$1.9$ is the 78\% unexplained variance of the $\theta$ fit, and is a further reason no $\theta$
exponent is claimed.
\item The pressure channel required a gauge and hydrostatic correction before the two members
could be compared (Sec.~\ref{sec:pressure}).
\end{itemize}
\subsection{The pressure channel: gauge and hydrostatic alignment}
\label{sec:pressure}

The two members store pressure in different conventions. The compressible raw pressure carries a
hydrostatic ramp $12\times$ larger than its dynamic part, and the two weak forms use opposite
sign conventions. Comparing raw pressures therefore measures the conventions rather than the flow:
the per-sample median of $|X_p|/|Y_p|$ is $0.065$, that of $\cos(X_p,Y_p)$ is $-0.105$, and the
$p$-channel ``law'' collapses to a constant ($R_p\approx1.0$). We align the members with the
protocol used by the public data set---subtract the mean, remove a least-squares linear-in-$y$ fit,
and flip the sign of the Boussinesq member. Under the same median convention the aligned pair gives
$0.960$ and $\cos=+0.991$, against $0.929$ and $+0.986$ for the public training set, so the two
pairings agree to within 3\%. The channel then follows the same law as the velocities
($A=0.148$, $m=1.009$). All $p$-channel numbers in this paper use this gauge. Pressure is
gauge-dependent: the exponent is a statement about this fixed post-processing gauge, not a
gauge-invariant law.

spatial field. A classical POD-ROM (44--52 modes, 99.9\% of the snapshot energy) whose coefficients
are regressed on $(\log_{10}Ra,\varepsilon)$ lands at 0.2937 with $\varepsilon$ and 0.3001 without,
i.e.\ at the level of doing nothing, and a purely linear surrogate that also sees the POD
coefficients of $X$ reaches only 0.2318. The plateau is not capacity---it is that the factor
scaling the quantity to be added is absent from the input.
\paragraph{A first-order perturbation proxy} If the correction is linear in $\varepsilon$, the
alternative is to solve the linearised compressible problem once per query and scale. That costs
one Jacobian assembly and one factorisation of the same discretisation. Timed on the Boussinesq
member (9.7\,s for a full march of 19 Newton iterations), one linear solve is estimated at
0.45\,s (wall time divided by Newton iterations; median over the 20 configurations, range
0.32--1.90\,s). Both $P$ and the network are identified
offline; the comparison here is per query. The field that solve produces is
$P=\lim_{\varepsilon\to0}D(\varepsilon)/\varepsilon$, the solution of $\mathcal{L}\delta=S_1[X]$.
We do not need a new solve to evaluate its accuracy: the paired scan already contains
$D(\varepsilon)$, so we estimate $P$ by third-order Richardson extrapolation in $\varepsilon$
over the solutions at $\varepsilon=0.4,0.8,1.2$. With $\mathcal{P}(\varepsilon)=D(\varepsilon)/\varepsilon$,
$P=3\mathcal{P}(0.4)-3\mathcal{P}(0.8)+\mathcal{P}(1.2)$, which has $O(\varepsilon^3)$ truncation and the same norm as the
$\varepsilon=0.4$ secant to within 1\% in the median over the 20 configurations, in all four
channels. The accuracy below is that of this extrapolated field. Its cost is estimated from the
measured march time divided by the number of Newton iterations (0.45\,s median, range
0.32--1.90\,s), not from a separately timed factorisation; it is an estimate of one linear solve,
not a measurement. We use it only as an order-of-magnitude comparison, not as a measured linear
solve. Predicting $\hat Y=X+\varepsilon P$ gives
$14$/$15$/$1.4$/$15\%$ at $\varepsilon=1.6$ and $24$/$27$/$3.0$/$28\%$ at $\varepsilon=1.9$
($u_x/u_y/\theta/p$), against a do-nothing floor at $\varepsilon=1.9$ of $39$/$58$/$6.0$/$42\%$: the linear response
removes 34--53\% of the gap at $\varepsilon=1.9$ (computed from the unrounded errors; $p$ gives
34.9\%) and leaves the rest to the $O(\varepsilon^2)$ term.
The secant at $\varepsilon=0.4$ predicts $22$/$26$/$2.9$/$26\%$ at $\varepsilon=1.9$, slightly
better than the linear response itself, because the secant already contains part of the quadratic
term; it costs one full compressible solve (30.3\,s median on this 20-configuration scan; 22.3\,s
is the 600-sample median of Table~\ref{tab:cost}) and using it as the linear baseline would
flatter that baseline. The network reaches
$9.4$/$10.6$/$1.4$/$11.3\%$ on the extrapolation split (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}), so the residual
left by the linear response is not a small correction. Both approaches replace the same compressible
member and therefore save a similar fraction of the 30.3\,s nonlinear solve; the first-order solve
cannot produce the $\varepsilon^2$ term, while inference costs 6.3\,ms against 0.45\,s per linear
solve.

\paragraph{A two-term fit, where it is available} The same low-$\varepsilon$ pairs allow a stronger
data-driven baseline: fit $\mathcal{P}(\varepsilon)=P+\varepsilon Q$ on $\varepsilon=0.4,0.8,1.2$ for each
configuration and predict $X+\varepsilon P+\varepsilon^2Q$. At $\varepsilon=1.6$ this gives
$3.3$/$3.9$/$0.4$/$3.8\%$ and at $\varepsilon=1.9$ $11.4$/$13.9$/$1.7$/$13.8\%$
($u_x/u_y/\theta/p$), against $14$/$15$/$1.4$/$15\%$ and $24$/$27$/$3.0$/$28\%$ for the
first-order response. That is the same order as the network's $9.4$/$10.6$/$1.4$/$11.3\%$,
averaged over the extrapolation split; the two are not evaluated on the same samples. The two-term fit is available only when paired
low-$\varepsilon$ data exist for the target flow. The public-data problem of Sec.~\ref{sec:public}
has no such pairs at the target contrast; there a single-$\varepsilon$ data set is all that exists,
and the prior---not a quadratic fit---is what makes it reusable.

\subsection{The physics prior: $D=\varepsilon P(X)$}
\label{sec:prior}

Same backbone, only the output structure changes (Table~\ref{tab:ablation} of
Sec.~\ref{sec:baselines}). B keeps the target $D$ and reads $\varepsilon$ from a constant input
channel; P1 reads $\varepsilon$ as well but trains on $D/\varepsilon$ and multiplies $\varepsilon$
back outside; P3 removes the $\varepsilon$ input entirely.



\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{fig3_prior.png}
\caption{The prior ablation for extrapolation (left) and interpolation (right). A: $\varepsilon$-blind.
B: $\varepsilon$ as input. P1: $\varepsilon$ in the network, multiplied outside. P2: learned
$(\varepsilon+\kappa\varepsilon^2)$ factor. P3: $\varepsilon$ never enters the network.}
\label{fig:prior}
\end{figure}

Three things happen at once (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}, Fig.~\ref{fig:prior}). In extrapolation the
factorised output beats a plain network that reads $\varepsilon$ from a constant input channel by
$1.61\times$ ($u_x$) and $1.59$--$1.81\times$ in the other channels, and the quadratic form adds a
further 7.1--10.6\% on top. That channel is a weak way to condition a network. A FiLM-conditioned
U-Net, which modulates every block with $\varepsilon$, reaches $0.1001\slash0.1155\slash0.0164\slash0.1251$ in
extrapolation and $0.0544\slash0.0496\slash0.0073\slash0.0428$ in interpolation, so against that baseline the channel-averaged error of the
factorised output is $1.09\times$ lower in extrapolation ($1.16\times$ in $\theta$ and $1.11\times$
in $p$ in the fixed gauge; the $u_x$ and $u_y$ intervals include zero), while
the two are indistinguishable in interpolation. Removing $\varepsilon$ from the input as well costs accuracy relative to P1 and FiLM---0.1214
against 0.0937 and 0.1001 in $u_x$---but still beats the $\varepsilon$-blind network
($1.87\times$ channel-averaged on the same 600-sample scan; $1.89\times$ in $u_x$); what it buys is the property P1 cannot have, a network that can be trained at
one $\varepsilon$ and applied at another (Sec.~\ref{sec:transfer}). Finally, the fitted curvature of the quadratic form is
positive, $\kappa=0.22$--$0.42$, the same sign and order as
the $\varepsilon^2$ term of the expansion in Sec.~\ref{sec:theory}---an independent check of the
asymptotic expansion.

\paragraph{Practical form} The prior changes only the training target: train any regressor $\mathcal{N}$ on the
ratio $D/\varepsilon$ instead of on $D$, and at run time return $\hat Y=X+\varepsilon\,\mathcal{N}(X)$
after evaluating the Boussinesq member (or a surrogate for it) to obtain $X$. Supplying
$\varepsilon$ to $\mathcal{N}$ as well is the most accurate form (P1, $1.09\times$ lower channel-averaged error than a FiLM-conditioned network in
extrapolation, with $\theta$ and $p$ individually significant and the $u_x/u_y$ intervals
including zero). P2 learns a scalar curvature $\kappa$ and replaces $\varepsilon$ by
$\varepsilon(1+\kappa\varepsilon)$ before the multiplication. Leaving the input out (P3) costs
accuracy at fixed data but is what lets one network serve
every contrast for which the prefactor is unchanged, because $\mathcal{N}$ then sees only $X$ and
re-learns the prefactor from the flow rather than from a tabulated value.

A Fourier neural operator (1.19\,M parameters) shows the same pattern against its
$\varepsilon$-blind version: $u_x$ $0.2287\to0.1334$ with the prior and $\varepsilon$
multiplied back outside the network ($1.71\times$), statistically indistinguishable from an
explicit $\varepsilon$ input channel ($0.1334\pm0.0004$ against $0.1342\pm0.0008$). On $u_y$ and
$\theta$ the prior is in fact better (0.1387 against 0.1456; 0.0177 against 0.0191); only on $p$
does the $\varepsilon$ channel win (0.1536 against 0.1734). The prior's value is therefore not that
it beats an $\varepsilon$-aware model---it is that it matches one while needing no $\varepsilon$ in
the input (Secs.~\ref{sec:learning}, \ref{sec:transfer}).

\subsection{Data efficiency}
\label{sec:learning}

The learning curve is tabulated in Table~S4 of the supplementary material and plotted in
Fig.~\ref{fig:learning}.

The naive expectation---that a structured model trades in-distribution accuracy for
extrapolation---is wrong at small $n$. At $n=50$ the prior is $1.4\times$ better than the
$\varepsilon$-input network ($u_x$ 0.1308 against 0.1831, a $9\sigma$ separation across three seeds),

400 in $u_x$ and $u_y$ (log-linear interpolation: $n\approx315$ and 360, i.e.\ near 300 in $u_x$),
essentially at $n=200$ in $p$, and $\theta$ never crosses---at $n=400$ it is still $2.5\sigma$
behind. Concretely, the prior at $n=200$ reaches what the $\varepsilon$-input network needs
$\approx270$ samples ($u_x$) and 290 ($u_y$) to reach. So the prior is two things at once: the
cheaper model when data is scarce, and---a model that needs no
$\varepsilon$ in its input, which is what makes a single-$\varepsilon$ data set reusable.

\subsection{What the correction does to the wall heat transfer}
\label{sec:nu}

Every error quoted so far is a field error. For a heat-transfer application the quantity that
matters is the Nusselt number, and the public classical cavity answers the question directly at
$\varepsilon=1.2$, because the two members share mesh and boundary conditions. We compute
$Nu=-\langle\partial\theta/\partial n\rangle$ at the hot wall with a six-point one-sided stencil.
On the Boussinesq member the same flux can be obtained without any wall stencil, from the
horizontally integrated flux $\int[u_x\theta-(Ra\,Pr)^{-1/2}\partial_x\theta]\,dy$, which is
independent of $x$ to $0.1\%$ at the three interior planes $x=0.25,0.5,0.75$; the wall estimate
agrees with it to $0.35\%$, which indicates the discretisation error of the estimator; the integral
change reported below is $1.5$ times that error.

The two statements are different in kind. The integral heat transfer is insensitive: the
compressible member carries more heat in all 200 samples, by $0.01$--$0.94\%$ (median $0.58\%$,
10--90\% range $0.06$--$0.92\%$). The $+0.54\%$ of Table~\ref{tab:nu} is the mean of the per-sample ratios at the six-point
stencil, and the ratio of the ensemble means is $+0.67\%$. Across two- to eight-point stencils the
per-sample mean spans $+0.35$ to $+0.62\%$ and the ratio of the ensemble means $+0.30$ to
$+0.90\%$. The local wall flux is not: the correction redistributes
it by $4.25\%$ in $L_1$ (p90 $7.33\%$), with pointwise excursions of $\pm10\%$, and that number is
the same for every stencil order. On average the correction adds heat over the lower half of the
cavity and removes it over the upper half (Fig.~\ref{fig:wallflux}b); the integral is the
$Nu$-weighted mean of this redistribution, which is why it cancels. A surrogate trained on
Boussinesq data therefore predicts the total heat load to better than $1\%$ at this contrast and
the local flux to only about $4\%$; which of the two matters is set by the application, not by the
physics. The field statistic behind the cancellation is large: on the same 200 samples the
temperature field itself differs by $8.4\%$ in relative $L_2$, so an $8.4\%$ change in the field
produces a $0.6\%$ change in its wall-normal derivative.

The gap grows with contrast. On the 20 configurations that carry five contrasts the mean of the per-sample
relative gaps is $+0.11\%$ at $\varepsilon=0.4$, $+0.44\%$ at 0.8, $+1.09\%$ at 1.2, $+2.42\%$ at
1.6 and $+5.06\%$ at 1.9, a log--log slope of 2.4; individual flows scatter around it (at
$\varepsilon=1.9$ the 10--90\% range is $-6.1$ to $+23\%$), because the sign of the local
redistribution differs between flows. The 20-configuration mean is therefore a second-order effect
that reaches $5.1\%$ at $\varepsilon=1.9$, but it is not zero and it is not uniform: at $Ra=10^6$
the four-family mean is $+28.5\%$ and individual families reach $+81.8\%$, while at $Ra\le10^3$ it
stays below $1\%$. A Boussinesq-trained surrogate cannot produce any of it: the saving over the
compressible solve is bought by keeping the compressible correction, not by replacing it with a
cheaper Boussinesq run.

\paragraph{The Rayleigh-number dependence of the integral correction} The aggregate $+0.54\%$
(median $+0.58\%$) is reproduced by the classical-cavity family at
$\varepsilon=1.2$, whose five Rayleigh numbers give $+0.01\%$ to $+0.87\%$
(mean $+0.51\%$; these classical-only values are not the four-family means of
Table~\ref{tab:nu_ra}). The dependence on $Ra$ and on the flow appears at larger
contrast. At $Ra\le10^3$ every family stays within $1\%$. At $\varepsilon=1.9$ the classical cavity
spans $-5.1\%$ to $-1.5\%$ over its five Rayleigh numbers ($-1.5\%$ at $Ra=10^6$), while the four
families at $Ra=10^6$ span $-1.5\%$ to
$+81.8\%$ around a mean of $+28.5\%$: the integral correction is set by the flow, not by
$\varepsilon$ alone, and an aggregate over families can hide an $80\%$ effect. A model calibrated on
$Nu$ alone would therefore not transfer across $Ra$ at fixed $\varepsilon$; the local field is the
quantity that carries the correction.

Three readings, in order of how much they matter for practice.

\begin{itemize}
\item Within the family, the prior is what makes a single-$\varepsilon$ data set reusable.
The identical architecture, budget and data give 0.1443 ($u_x$) with the plain output and 0.0932
with the prior---a factor 1.55---and $3.2\times$ better than doing nothing, on $\varepsilon$ the
data never contained. It is even better than the same prior trained on our own 382 samples
(0.1250), because it sees ten times more configurations. This is the concrete content of ``the
$\varepsilon$ dependence need not be in the data''.
\item The prior also fixes the failure mode of the plain model. The plain model's error at
unseen $\varepsilon$ is largest where the training $\varepsilon$ is furthest away (its correction
is a fixed field, Sec.~\ref{sec:floor}), whereas the prior's error stays proportional to how wrong
$P$ is, uniformly in $\varepsilon$.
\item Across boundary-condition families the prior does not rescue transfer. Trained on the
classical cavity, the plain model lands at 0.2919 against a floor of 0.3018---it learned little that
transfers---and in extrapolation the prior is worse than the plain model in all four channels
(0.3092/0.4730/0.0561/0.3280 against 0.2919/0.3711/0.0490/0.3192). In interpolation the prior does
beat the plain model in all four channels (0.2281/0.3498/0.0422/0.2584 against
0.2444/0.3612/0.0484/0.3217), but it still does not beat doing nothing. The failure is the one
Sec.~\ref{sec:public} predicts: $A$ is a functional of the flow, and a surrogate trained on one
family does not know another family's $A$. The classical cavity's peak Boussinesq speed is 41\%
above the random-wall median, so $\hat u$ separates the two families; it does not, however, order
the per-sample error within the public random-wall splits under the per-channel relative $L_2$
metric used here (Spearman $-0.20$; partial correlation $0.09$ at fixed $Ra$). We therefore use
$\hat u$ as a family-level diagnostic, not as a within-family transfer law.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Solver noise is not the source of the law}
\label{sec:noise}

\begin{itemize}
\item Convergence quality over 600 samples (compressible member): median relative change
$3.75\times10^{-6}$, p90 $1.09\times10^{-4}$, worst $7.04\times10^{-4}$.
\item Dropping the 65 samples with relative change $>10^{-4}$ changes the linear $u_x$ fit from
$0.1809\varepsilon-0.0212$ to $0.1813\varepsilon-0.0221$---unchanged. The negative intercept is a
property of this linear fit; the power-law fit of Table~\ref{tab:law} has an intercept consistent
with zero.
\item Re-running the 10 worst samples with \path{steady_tol} tightened from $10^{-6}$ to
$10^{-8}$ leaves the solution bit-identical ($|\Delta Y|/|Y|=0$). The reason is
structural: the compressible march terminates on the zero-iteration guard, not on
\path{steady_tol}, so the residual reported is the fixed-point residual of the discrete solution,
not unfinished iteration.
\end{itemize}

Table~\ref{tab:cost} gives the wall-clock cost. A paired solve splits into a Boussinesq member
(median 11.7\,s) and a compressible member (22.3\,s); the surrogate replaces only the second. Per
pair the cost therefore falls from 33.9\,s to 11.7\,s, a factor $2.9$, and the comparison of one
inference with one compressible solve ($22.3\,\mathrm{s}/6.3\,\mathrm{ms}=3.5\times10^3$) describes only that member.
The amortised regime depends on how many flows are swept, because $X$ does not depend on
$\varepsilon$. For a single flow and $N=100$ contrasts, one Boussinesq solve plus 100 inferences
costs $16.0+0.63=16.6$\,s against $16.0+100\times37.5=3.77\times10^3$\,s, a factor
$2.3\times10^2$. The scan measured here is 20 flows $\times$ 5 contrasts and was run in a separate session, 1.7 times
slower per sample; its own timings are
16.0\,s for the Boussinesq member and 37.5\,s for the compressible one; the median of their
per-pair sum is 55.8\,s. On the same-reuse basis used above, the baseline is
$20\times16.0+100\times37.5=4.07\times10^3$\,s and the surrogate needs
$20\times16.0+100\times0.0063=3.21\times10^2$\,s, a factor $13$. If the baseline is instead the
scan as actually run, which repeats the Boussinesq solve at every contrast,
$100\times55.8=5.58\times10^3$\,s, the ratio is $17$; we quote the conservative same-reuse
$13\times$ in the abstract. The solver runs on eight CPU threads and the surrogate on a GPU; the
two are not the same hardware.

\subsection{A third Prandtl number: $Pr=4.38$}
\label{sec:water}

$Pr$ and $\varepsilon$ do not mix. $Pr$ enters the coefficients of both members
($Re^{-1}=(Pr/Ra)^{1/2}$) and hence the Boussinesq solution $X$, but the $\varepsilon$-expansion of
Sec.~\ref{sec:theory} never multiplies the two. The prediction is therefore sharp and asymmetric:
the exponent must not move, while the prefactor may, because $X$ itself has changed.

We generated 104 further pairs at $Pr=4.38$, the Prandtl number of water near $40^\circ$C. The
generator changes $Pr$ at fixed $Ra$ in the dimensionless formulation, so $Re=(Ra/Pr)^{1/2}$ and
the thermal diffusivity change with it; the equation of state and $\gamma$ are those of the air
run. The study isolates the $Pr$ axis and does not claim water properties
(Table~\ref{tab:water}, Fig.~\ref{fig:water}).
The pairs are bit-identical to
the first 104 samples of the main batch in every other respect: same seed stream, hence the same
wall-temperature Fourier draws, the same $Ra$ and the same $\varepsilon$. The two families are
therefore a strict paired experiment in which $Pr$ is the only changed parameter.

$Pr$ is therefore not a break-symmetry axis either, and the law's two ingredients separate
cleanly: $\varepsilon$ sets the exponent, the flow sets the prefactor, and the fluid changes neither
by more than 3.3\% for $u_x$, $u_y$ and $\theta$ and 6.1\% for $p$ on the paired comparison. Keeping

than the geometry (Sec.~\ref{sec:aspect}) or the boundary-condition family
(Sec.~\ref{sec:public}), which move $A$ by up to $4\times$.
\subsection{A three-dimensional check}
\label{sec:3d}

The law is calibrated in two dimensions. As a preliminary check of whether the exponent survives in
three, we generated fifteen paired steady solutions with the same generator: three fixed flows---a three-cell
Voronoi wall pair, a two-cell wall pair and a constant-wall cavity---each solved at
$\varepsilon=0.2$, $0.4$, $0.8$, $1.2$ and $1.6$, with $Ra=10^4$, $Pr=0.71$ and $res=16$ ($33^3$ velocity nodes,
$17^3$ temperature and pressure nodes). The pairing is exact: within each flow the wall temperatures
are the same seed stream scaled affinely about $T_0$, so the low-fidelity member is the same
solution up to the $\varepsilon$-blind rescaling of Sec.~\ref{sec:blind}.
For the pressure channel we use the same fixed gauge as in two dimensions: subtract the mean,
remove a linear fit in $y$ (the gravity direction), and flip the sign; the horizontal $z$ direction
is not deramped. The $p$ exponents below are therefore fixed-gauge numbers, as in
Table~\ref{tab:law}.

Table~\ref{tab:3d} and Fig.~\ref{fig:3d} give the result. In every channel and every flow a
power law fits the five $\varepsilon$ values in log--log coordinates with $R^2>0.995$, and the per-flow exponents are
$1.091\pm0.014$ ($u_x$), $1.052\pm0.021$ ($u_y$), $1.130\pm0.015$ ($\theta$) and
$1.141\pm0.017$ ($p$)---within $0.16$ of unity. Against the full-window 2-D fit of
Table~\ref{tab:law} the flow means differ by $0.07$ ($u_x$), $0.06$ ($u_y$) and $0.13$ ($p$);
against a 2-D fit restricted to the same $\varepsilon=0.2$--$1.6$ window ($1.04/0.95/1.20/1.08$ for $u_x/u_y/\theta/p$,
485 samples) the differences are $0.05/0.11/0.07/0.06$. The second comparison is noisier because a narrow-window regression there
also measures the between-flow prefactor scatter (up to $4\times$, Sec.~\ref{sec:public}). Both are
consistency checks, not a converged validation. The prefactor is not universal here either: across the three flows it varies
by $3.8\times$ ($u_x$), $2.1\times$ ($u_y$), $11\times$ ($\theta$) and $2.0\times$ ($p$), which is
the same statement as Sec.~\ref{sec:public}, that $A$ is a functional of the flow. What transfers to
three dimensions is the exponent, which is what the factorisation predicts.
With fifteen samples at a single $Ra$ and a single resolution, this is a consistency check, not a
converged three-dimensional validation.

\paragraph{A POD-ROM check of the prior} The same fifteen samples also support a
linear surrogate check. We train a POD-ROM with at most four modes on three flows and four
contrasts (twelve samples) and test on the held-out contrast (three samples),
repeating for each of the five contrasts. The plain ROM predicts $D$ from
$[1,\varepsilon,\bm c_X]$, where $\bm c_X$ are at most four POD coefficients of the
Boussinesq field; the prior ROM predicts $P=D/\varepsilon$ from
$[1,\bm c_X]$ and multiplies by $\varepsilon$ outside. At most four modes are used,
fewer than the twelve training samples. Averaged over the five held-out contrasts,
the prior lowers the relative error in every channel (Table~\ref{tab:3drom}):
$u_x$ from 0.168 to 0.081, $u_y$ from 0.190 to 0.150, $\theta$ from 0.034 to
0.027 and $p$ from 0.163 to 0.098. The gain is largest at $\varepsilon=0.2$ and
0.4 ($14\times$ and $5\times$ in $u_x$) and is not uniform: at $\varepsilon=1.2$ the
$\varepsilon$-aware plain ROM is better in all four channels. We read this as a
check that the factorisation remains useful in three dimensions at small
contrast, not as a claim of uniform superiority.


The check is limited to $Ra=10^4$, $res=16$ and $\varepsilon\le1.6$. The fifteen pairs cost
12--32\,min each (median 16\,min) of outer wall-clock time on this machine. We therefore report the exponent transfer, not
a three-dimensional sweep.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Two dimensions for the main sweep. The law is calibrated on 2-D solutions; a $2\times$ mesh
refinement moves the exponent by less than $0.005$ at $Ra=10^4$ and by at most $0.015$ at
$Ra=10^6$, where the largest pointwise change in $R$ is $3.3\%$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:law}). The preliminary
15-sample 3-D check of Sec.~\ref{sec:3d} agrees with the 2-D exponent within $0.16$ over the
same $\varepsilon=0.2$--$1.6$ window, but covers one Rayleigh number,
$res=16$ and $\varepsilon\le1.6$; the POD-ROM baseline is a linear model with at most four modes, not a deep operator. Each 3-D pair costs 12--32\,min of outer wall-clock time (median 16) on this machine. A
3-D sweep at higher $Ra$ or resolution remains out of reach here.
\item $Ma$ is inert here, so the break-symmetry group is one-dimensional rather than two.
At larger $Ma$ this would have to be redone.
\item $\varepsilon<2$, imposed by the equation of state (the cold wall must stay positive).
\item The power-law fits of Sec.~\ref{sec:law} leave 16/18/78\% of the variance unexplained in
$u_x/u_y/\theta$ and 13\% in $p$; the $\chi$ form of Sec.~\ref{sec:law} raises $R^2$ from $0.19$ to
$0.78$ and the $\hat u$ form of Sec.~\ref{sec:public} to $0.90$, and the same residual
flow-dependence remains in the other three channels.
\item $\theta$ is not part of the common-exponent claim. Its free power-law fit is not identifiable
at this sample size: $R^2=0.22$, slope $1.024$ over the full window and $1.67$ on the
104-configuration subset, and the fitted amplitude is not predictive ($0.012$ at
$\varepsilon=1.2$ against a public median of $0.0228$). We make no exponent claim for $\theta$.
\item The pressure channel is quoted in the fixed gauge and hydrostatic correction of
Sec.~\ref{sec:pressure}. Its exponent is not gauge-invariant, and a different post-processing
gauge would change it.
\item The prefactor $A$ is configuration-dependent and must be learned: it moves with the geometry
(Sec.~\ref{sec:aspect}) and by $1.66\times$ ($u_x$) / $1.38\times$ ($u_y$) / $4.05\times$
($\theta$) / $1.07\times$ ($p$) between boundary-condition families of an independent data set
(Sec.~\ref{sec:public}), while it is insensitive to the fluid ($Pr$, Sec.~\ref{sec:water}).
\item The earlier public release used a pinned $Fr=1.095$, which puts its
$Ra_{\mathrm{eff}}=Ra\,Fr^2/\varepsilon$ 0.08\% below ours at $\varepsilon=1.2$
($1.2/1.199=1.0008$); the v2 release corrects this and the two now agree.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We set out to build a surrogate for compressible natural convection and found that the object it has
to reproduce---the gap between the Boussinesq solution and the compressible one---factorises:
$\varepsilon$ sets its leading-order scaling, and the flow sets its amplitude and spatial shape.

\begin{enumerate}
\item One break-symmetry parameter. In this non-dimensionalisation $Fr^2=\Delta T/T_0$ identically,
so the break-symmetry axis is $\varepsilon=\Delta T/T_0$; Mach-number effects are $O(Ma^2)$ and
are below the resolution of the sampled range. This does not make the amplitude universal:
$P(X)$ carries the flow dependence.
\item A leading-order law. $R=\|Y-X\|/\|Y\|=A\varepsilon^m$ with
$m=1.023\pm0.018$ and $0.995\pm0.019$ ($u_x,u_y$), an
intercept indistinguishable from zero, the exponent quoted over the full two-decade window (from
$\varepsilon=0.8$ upwards the same points give $1.23$--$1.32$, the $\varepsilon^2$ curvature of
Sec.~\ref{sec:law}), and a 134-family boundary-condition description (133 dummies) that adds only
2--3 pp of adjusted $R^2$ to the velocity and pressure channels. The fit leaves 16/18/78/13\% of
the variance unexplained in $u_x/u_y/\theta/p$; $\theta$ collapses on $\chi$ rather than on
$\varepsilon$ alone. The pressure channel gives $m=1.009\pm0.015$ in the fixed gauge of
Sec.~\ref{sec:pressure}; this is a numerical observation, not a gauge-invariant exponent.
$\theta$ is a weak channel with no identifiable exponent: its free fit gives $1.024$ over the full
window and $1.67$ on the 104-configuration subset, and its fitted amplitude is not predictive
($0.012$ at $\varepsilon=1.2$ against a public median of $0.0228$). For $u_x$ and $u_y$ the
exponent is consistent across geometry ($0.7$ and $1.8$ standard errors) and fluid (1.5\%). Only the
prefactor $A$ must be calibrated, which is why the surrogate takes $X$ as input rather than a
table of $A$. A preliminary set of fifteen paired 3-D solutions at one $Ra$ and one resolution
reproduces the exponent (flow means $1.05$--$1.14$, per-flow $1.03$--$1.15$; Sec.~\ref{sec:3d}).
\item A reason. The $\varepsilon$-expansion of the compressible member reproduces the
Boussinesq system at $O(1)$ and produces the correction through
$\nabla\cdot\bm u_1=\nabla\cdot(\theta_0\bm u_0)$ and the density-modulated inertia. The linear
operator of that system does not contain $\varepsilon$, so the correction factorises,
$D=\varepsilon P(X)+\varepsilon^2Q(X)+\dots$, with a shape that does not rotate. All five
consequences of the expansion listed in Table~S1 are confirmed numerically.
\item A design rule. Predicting $P(X)$ and multiplying by $\varepsilon$ outside the
network, which changes only the target and one multiplication, converts the law into a prior that
(i) has $1.09\times$ lower channel-averaged error than a FiLM-conditioned network in extrapolation
($\theta$ and $p$ individually, $p$ in the fixed gauge; the $u_x/u_y$ intervals include zero), (ii) lets the network
discover the $\varepsilon^2$ term itself,
(iii) works the same way for a Fourier neural operator, and (iv) makes a public data set that exists
at a single temperature contrast reusable at others---0.093 ($u_x$) on $\varepsilon>1.3$ against
0.144 for the plain model and 0.302 for doing nothing---which is the difference between a surrogate
that must be retrained and one that can be applied. The scope of that reuse is set by
Sec.~\ref{sec:public}: it holds within a boundary-condition family and fails across one,
predictably.
\end{enumerate}

The same factorisation lowers the mean $u_x$ error of a POD-ROM with at most four modes on the 3-D samples from 0.168 to 0.081 over five held-out contrasts.

Section~\ref{sec:limits} lists the limits: two dimensions for the main sweep, $\varepsilon<2$,
one-dimensional break symmetry, and a prefactor whose power-law fit leaves 16\% ($u_x$), 18\%
($u_y$) and 78\% ($\theta$) of the variance unexplained; the $\theta$ gap closes to 22\% once the
advection strength $\chi$ is supplied. The leading $\varepsilon$ factor is derived; the amplitude,
the window-averaged exponent and the $\varepsilon^2$ curvature are measured, and the curvature is
also learned by the network.
